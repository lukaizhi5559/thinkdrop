What is Frame Semantics and Why Does It Matter for Bible Study?
Frame semantics is the idea that words don’t exist in isolation—they evoke entire conceptual
frames made up of roles, relationships, and background knowledge. When we read Scripture,
words like “rest,
” “sacrifice,
” or “Satan” carry layers of meaning shaped by ancient cultural,
theological, and narrative contexts. A frame semantic approach helps us see the richness and
interconnectedness of biblical language, revealing how meaning is found not just in the word
itself, but in the frame it activates. This can also help us see the Bible in its ancient light versus
how we currently understand terms and ideas. It’s a powerful tool for studying the Bible on its
own terms—contextually, relationally, and holistically.

Lexical Unit: A specific word or phrase that is used in a specific context (e.g.,
Genesis 5:29) that activates a frame.

Frame: The mental structure or storyline behind a word—like a “container” that holds the
frame elements or a “scene” that gives the word its meaning.
Frame Elements: The key roles or parts of that frame—people, actions, settings,
goals—that the word assumes (e.g., giver of relief, recipient, burden, place of rest).
Semantic Roles: Broad functional roles that participants play within an event (e.g.,
agent, experiencer, theme); these describe “who is doing what to/with whom.
”
Today, we often use truth conditional semantics to determine meaning and truth.
The contrast between truth conditionals and frames:
Truth-conditional semantics asks what must be true for a statement to be accurate; frame
semantics asks what background structures must be in place for a word to be meaningful.

Step-by-Step Guide: How to Use Frame Semantics
An example from Genesis 5:29
“And he called his name Noah, saying,
'This one will give us relief from our work and from the
toil of our hands, from the ground which YHWH has cursed.
” (Genesis 5:29, my translation)
1. Identify the lexical unit (word or phrase in context/actual use):
→ “Relief” (Hebrew: naḥam)
The use suggests more than physical rest—implying change, an emotional or existential relief.
2. Ask: What kind of scene or frame is this word evoking?
→ A Toil-and-Relief Frame that overlaps with the Divine Rest Frame: a world marked by
cursed ground and painful labor, paired with a longing for comfort, renewal, and a return to
God’s intended order.
3. Name or describe the frame:
A frame involving burden, divine curse, human suffering, and hope for someone to
reverse or alleviate that burden.
4. Identify the frame elements (roles in the scene):
Frame Element Textual Example
Burden Toil, cursed ground
Experiencer Humanity (those doing the toil)
Giver of relief Noah (his name means “rest”)
Source of burden God’s curse on the ground
Result (hoped for) Comfort, ease, perhaps reversal
5. Observe how the frame shapes meaning:
→ This isn’t “relief” as a nap or vacation or even a temporary reprieve. It’s prophetic hope for
reversal of the curse—a theological frame of burden, redemption, and divine intervention that
anticipates or participates in the broader Divine Rest Frame, where God restores creation
through rest, presence, and covenant.

Before You Begin: Tips for Frame-Based Interpretation
What to Consider When Analyzing a Frame
1. Literary Context Matters
Don’t isolate the verse—look at what comes before and after. How does this passage build on
or contrast with the surrounding material?
2. Cultural and Theological Memory
Ask what background stories or categories the author assumes the reader knows—like
covenant, temple, exile, divine council, etc. What kind of a world do they live in?
3. Avoid Anachronism
Keep the meaning grounded in the world of the text. Don’t import modern definitions or ideas
(e.g.,
“Satan” as a red devil, or “rest” as a lazy afternoon playing video games).
4. You Don’t Have to Name the Frame Right Away
Sometimes you’ll sense a “scene” before you have a label for it. Describe the relationships and
roles first—then try to name the frame!
5. Frame Elements Are Roles and States, Not Just People and Things
A “role” might be a person, object, or even an abstract idea—like “burden,
” “place of presence,
”
or “symbol of authority.
”

from episode 121 of Genesis Marks the Spot
What Is the Divine Rest Frame?
A theological frame in which God brings the created order from toil, curse, and chaos into rest,
presence, and restoration.
Lexical Units That Activate This Frame
●
●
●
●
●
●
●
Rest (nuach, anapausis)
Relief (revach, yeshuah)
Sabbath (shabbat)
Inheritance
Land (as place of rest)
Dwelling / Tabernacle / Temple
Shalom
Core Frame Elements
Frame Element Example from Passage
Divine Agent / Giver God, YHWH, Jesus
Recipients Israel, humanity, the weary, the land itself
Burden / Toil Labor, slavery, exile, sin, death
Relief / Rest Physical rest, spiritual renewal, covenant fulfillment
Time 7th day, Sabbath, Jubilee, Messianic age
Place Garden, Promised land, tabernacle, temple, New Jerusalem
Sign / Symbol Sabbath, divine presence, temple, Body of Christ
Outcome Restoration, presence, worship, peace, new creation
Theological Function
The Divine Rest Frame reveals how rest is not simply cessation of work, but the goal of
creation, the center of covenant life, and the promise of final restoration. It connects creation,
redemption, and eschatology into a single theological vision of divine order and peace.
